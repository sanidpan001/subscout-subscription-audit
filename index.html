    The Complete Guide to Running a Subscription Audit (and Keeping the Savings)

    Subscriptions are the quietest line item in your budget. A streaming plan here, a cloud-storage plan there, a meditation app you opened twice in January, a software tool your old job used to pay for — each one feels small on its own. Together, they form a slow financial leak that most people never fully measure. The question "how much do I spend on subscriptions" sounds simple, but almost nobody can answer it without sitting down and listing every recurring charge. That is exactly what a subscription audit is for, and a good subscription audit calculator turns that painful detective work into a ten-minute exercise with a clear verdict at the end: keep it, kill it, or think about it.

    What a subscription audit actually does

    A subscription audit is a structured review of every recurring payment leaving your accounts — monthly, quarterly, and yearly — with one goal: deciding whether each charge still earns its place. Unlike a generic budget review, an audit focuses on the peculiar psychology of subscriptions. They are designed to be forgotten. Free trials convert silently. Prices creep upward in small increments. Annual plans auto-renew while you are busy with something else. A proper subscription tracker with savings features does three things a spreadsheet cannot do as easily: it converts every billing cycle into a comparable monthly and yearly cost, it forces a usage honesty-check per service, and it projects the long-term damage of keeping each one. The last part matters most. A $12.99 monthly plan is not a $12.99 decision — it is a roughly $1,700 decision over ten years once you account for the price hikes that streaming and software companies reliably push through every couple of years.

    How much do I spend on subscriptions? The honest math

    The average household in many countries now carries between six and twelve paid subscriptions, and surveys consistently find that people underestimate their total subscription spending by a wide margin — often by half or more. The reason is not carelessness; it is fragmentation. Charges arrive on different cards, in different currencies, on different cycles. A yearly antivirus renewal feels like a one-off, not a subscription. A quarterly domain registration slips past the monthly mental budget entirely. To answer "how much do I spend on subscriptions" accurately, you need to normalize everything to a single time window: convert weekly plans to monthly (multiply by 52, divide by 12), quarterly plans to monthly (divide by 3), and yearly plans to monthly (divide by 12). Then add them up. That single number — your monthly subscription burn — is the foundation of the audit. The yearly total is the number that usually produces the gasp, because twelve months of "it is only ten bucks" is suddenly a four-figure sum.

    The Keep / Kill / Maybe verdict system

    Every subscription in a serious audit gets a verdict, and three buckets are enough. Keep is for services you use heavily relative to their cost — daily-use tools, the one streaming service your whole family actually watches, the cloud plan holding your business files. Kill is for the zombies: trials that converted, apps you have not opened in months, duplicate services (two music apps, three cloud drives), and anything whose cost-per-use has become absurd. Maybe is the honest middle ground — the seasonal service you binge for two months a year, the niche tool you use for one project each quarter. Maybe items are where the real savings hide, because they invite smarter tactics: pause instead of cancel, switch to seasonal activation, downgrade a tier, or share a family plan. A quality subscription audit calculator scores each subscription on usage frequency, price tier, and cost-per-use so the verdicts are consistent instead of vibes-based. The scoring in SubScout, for example, weights daily use heavily, penalizes high monthly prices, and penalizes a high cost-per-use — so a $3 monthly app you open daily can outscore a $20 monthly service you touch once a month.

    The subscription health score: one number for the whole mess

    Once every subscription has a verdict, you need one number that summarizes the entire picture. That is the subscription health score: a 0–100 rating where 100 means every dollar of recurring spend goes to services you genuinely use, and low scores mean a large share of your money feeds Kill-verdict zombies. The score should reflect three things. First, the share of your monthly spend sitting in Kill verdicts — this is the biggest drag on the score, because it is pure waste. Second, the share in Maybe verdicts, which is a smaller drag but signals inefficiency. Third, subscription count itself: beyond a handful of services, each additional subscription adds management overhead, renewal risk, and price-hike exposure, so a lean stack scores better than a sprawling one even at equal spend. Think of the health score the way you think of a credit score — not as a judgment, but as a single dial to improve month over month. Re-run your audit quarterly and watch the number climb as you cancel, pause, and consolidate.

    The annual-vs-monthly trap most people pay for

    Here is one of the least-discussed subscription rip-offs: monthly billing is frequently 15–25% more expensive per year than the annual plan for the exact same service, and companies rarely advertise the gap prominently. A $12.99 monthly plan costs $155.88 per year; the annual plan might be $119.99 — a $36 difference for identical access. The catch is psychological: monthly feels cheaper because the number is smaller, and it feels safer because canceling seems easier. For services with a firm Keep verdict — the ones you are certain to use all year — switching to annual billing is free money. For Maybe services, the math flips: annual plans are a trap if you will only use the service for three months, because the "discount" locks you into nine months of waste. The rule is simple and worth memorizing: annual billing for Keeps, monthly billing for Maybes you are testing, and cancellation for Kills. Run the numbers with an annual-vs-monthly billing optimizer before you commit, because some "annual discounts" are illusions — a few services price their annual plan above twelve months of the monthly rate, counting on customers not to multiply.

    Price-hike creep: the silent budget killer

    Subscriptions do not stay the price you signed up at. Streaming services, software suites, and cloud providers raise prices every 18–36 months, usually by a dollar or two — small enough that you shrug, frequent enough that it compounds. A service that cost $9.99 in 2020, $12.99 in 2022, and $15.49 in 2024 has a creep rate of roughly 11% per year, far above general inflation. Nobody tracks this in their head, which is why a price-hike creep log is one of the most valuable features a subscription tracker with savings can offer. Log the date and price of every increase, and over time you get two powerful numbers: the total percentage increase since you subscribed, and the annualized creep rate. The annualized rate is the honest one — it lets you compare a service that raised prices twice in five years against one that raised once in two years. When a service's creep rate exceeds 8–10% per year, treat it as a re-audit trigger: the value equation you accepted at signup no longer exists, and the service deserves a fresh Keep-or-Kill verdict at its new price.

    The 5-year savings projection: why small cancellations matter

    Humans discount the future aggressively. Saving $14.99 a month feels like pocket change, so canceling feels like a chore not worth the effort. The 5-year projection fixes this bias by showing the compounded truth. Kill-verdict subscriptions save 100% of their yearly cost, and Maybe-verdict services — assuming you cut half of them or halve their usage — save roughly half. Then apply a modest annual price-hike assumption, say 5%, because keeping a subscription means paying tomorrow's higher price, not today's. Under those assumptions, cutting $60/month of waste is not a $720 decision — it is a roughly $3,970 decision over five years. That is a vacation, an emergency fund top-up, or a serious dent in high-interest debt. The projection is what converts audit results into action, because it translates "meh, fourteen bucks" into a number your future self will thank you for.

    Advanced tactics after your first audit

    Once the obvious kills are done, the second wave of savings comes from strategy. Seasonal rotation beats stacking for entertainment: subscribe to one streaming service at a time, binge, cancel, rotate. Most platforms now make canceling easy because they know you will be back — use that. Tier audits catch over-provisioning: the premium 4K tier nobody in the house can distinguish from the standard tier, the 2TB cloud plan holding 90GB of files, the business software seat count from when the team was bigger. Family and bundle consolidation turns three individual plans into one shared plan at half the per-person cost. Trial hygiene prevents future zombies: the moment you start a free trial, create the renewal reminder immediately, and prefer virtual-card or calendar-blocked trials where available. And watch for duplicate coverage — the most common audit finding is two services doing the same job, like a music app plus a video bundle that includes music, or two cloud drives syncing the same folders. None of these tactics require willpower; they require a system, and a system is exactly what a recurring audit gives you.

    Why a browser-based audit tool beats a spreadsheet

    Spreadsheets work, but they have a fatal flaw: the formulas live in your head, so every audit session starts from scratch and the math is inconsistent. A dedicated subscription audit calculator encodes the methodology — cycle normalization, cost-per-use scoring, verdict thresholds, health-score weighting, savings projection with price-hike assumptions — so every audit uses the same rules and the results are comparable over time. Browser-based tools add two more advantages: your data never leaves your device, which matters when you are typing in what you pay for things, and the friction of starting is near zero. The best audit is the one you actually repeat, and a tool that remembers your subscriptions, your creep log, and your renewal reminders between sessions turns a one-time purge into a quarterly habit. That habit is the entire game. Subscription waste is not a one-time problem; it is a recurring leak, and only a recurring process plugs it.

    Frequently asked questions

    How does the subscription audit calculator decide Keep, Kill, or Maybe?+

    Each subscription is scored 0–100 from three inputs: how often you use it (daily use scores far higher than rare use), its monthly price tier, and its cost-per-use (monthly cost divided by uses per month). Scores of 65+ earn Keep, 40–64 earn Maybe, and below 40 earn Kill. Every verdict shows its reasoning so you can see exactly why a service landed where it did.

    How do I figure out how much I spend on subscriptions per year?+

    Add every recurring charge with its true billing cycle — weekly, monthly, quarterly, or yearly. SubScout normalizes each one to monthly and yearly equivalents (for example, a weekly plan is multiplied by 52 to get the yearly cost) and totals them. The yearly total is the eye-opening number: it reveals the real cost of "it's only a few bucks a month" thinking.

    Is annual billing always cheaper than monthly billing?+

    Not always. Many services discount annual plans 15–25% versus twelve months of monthly billing, but some price the annual plan at or even above the monthly-equivalent total. Use the annual-vs-monthly optimizer to compare both prices before committing — and only switch firm Keep verdicts to annual, since annual plans lock in services you might otherwise cancel.

    What is subscription price-hike creep, and why track it?+

    Price-hike creep is the slow rise of a subscription's price through small periodic increases — a dollar here, two dollars there. Individually the hikes feel trivial, but they often compound at 8–12% per year, far above inflation. Logging each increase lets you see the total change and the annualized creep rate, which tells you when a service's value equation has changed enough to deserve a fresh audit.

    How is the 5-year savings projection calculated?+

    Kill-verdict subscriptions contribute 100% of their yearly cost to the savings, and Maybe-verdict services contribute 50% (a conservative assumption that you cut or halve half of them). Each future year's savings grow by a 5% assumed price-hike rate, because keeping a subscription means paying tomorrow's higher prices. The total shows what the cuts are really worth over five years.

    Is my subscription data private? Where is it stored?+

    Yes — everything stays in your browser. SubScout stores your subscriptions and creep log in your browser's localStorage and never sends them to any server; there is no account, no tracking, and no database. The .ics renewal reminders are generated on your device and downloaded directly. Clear your browser data and the entries disappear with it.
